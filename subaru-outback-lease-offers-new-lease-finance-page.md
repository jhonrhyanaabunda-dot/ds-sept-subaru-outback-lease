## Lease or finance your subaru outback

The Subaru Outback is the all-wheel-drive wagon that built the category — standard Symmetrical All-Wheel Drive on every trim, 8.7 inches of ground clearance on the standard models, and a cargo hold that swallows a road bike with the front wheel still on. The question for most shoppers isn't whether to get one. It's how to pay for it.

Leasing and financing each fit a different driver, and our desk works both sides every day. Here's the honest version of how they split.

**Leasing an Outback** keeps your monthly payment lower and rotates you into a new vehicle every two or three years. It fits if you like the newest EyeSight safety tech, you drive predictable miles, and you'd rather not own a vehicle long-term. The catch — and this is the part a lot of buyers gloss over, is the mileage cap. A standard Subaru lease is quoted at 10,000 or 12,000 miles a year. If your commute from Chula Vista or the South Bay runs you past that, the per-mile overage charge at lease-end can erase the monthly savings you signed up for. last month our team reviewed a Chula Vista commuter's actual odometer history before quoting a lease, found she was logging closer to 18,000 miles a year, and steered her into finance instead, we'd rather lose the lease than hand you a bill at turn-in.

**Financing an Outback** is the path to ownership. You build equity, you drive as many miles as you want, and once it's paid off the payments stop. The U.S. Department of Energy's [fueleconomy.gov](https://www.fueleconomy.gov) rates a current non-turbo Outback at 26 city / 32 highway / 29 combined MPG, and because Subaru's horizontally opposed engines are known for going the distance, financing is the smarter math if you tend to keep a vehicle past the loan term. We've had original Dalton Subaru National City finance customers come back to trade Outbacks with well over 150,000 miles on the clock. Those owners almost always finance again.

There's no universally "right" answer. The right one depends on your annual mileage, how long you keep a car, and your monthly budget. That's exactly the conversation our team is built to have, and we'll run both numbers side by side so you're not guessing.

## Lease vs. finance: how the numbers differ

A lot of shoppers walk in fixed on one path before they've seen what the other one costs. Here's the framework we use at the desk to make it concrete, without quoting a payment that'll be stale next week.

A **lease payment** is built on three things: the capitalized cost (essentially the negotiated price), the residual value (what Subaru of America projects the Outback will be worth at lease-end), and the money factor (the lease equivalent of an interest rate). You're paying for the depreciation across your term, plus rent charge, not the whole car. That's why the monthly is lower. The Outback's strong residuals, which trace back to its safety scores and Subaru's reliability reputation, are the reason it leases better than a lot of competitors in its segment.

A **finance payment** is built on the full price, your down payment, the loan term, and the APR. You're buying the whole vehicle, so the monthly is higher, but every payment buys equity, and you own an asset at the end. For resale and trade-in context, independent valuation sources like Kelley Blue Book ([kbb.com](https://www.kbb.com)) and Edmunds ([edmunds.com](https://www.edmunds.com)) publish Outback residual and resale data you can sanity-check our finance math against. We encourage it, a customer who's checked KBB before they sit down is a customer we can close honestly and fast.

The practical rule we give people: if you'll keep the Outback five-plus years and drive real miles, finance. If you want the newest tech every few years and your mileage is genuinely under the cap, lease. Everyone in the middle should let us run both and look at the total cost, not the monthly.

## What most buyers get wrong about Lease-End

The single biggest surprise at turn-in is wear-and-tear and mileage charges, and it's avoidable. A Subaru lease holds you to a mileage cap and a normal-wear standard. We've had customers roll in 4,000 miles over and owe real money, money that, in hindsight, a finance deal would have spared them entirely.

So before you sign a lease, do two things. First, be honest about your driving. California's average is roughly 12,000–13,000 miles a year per the U.S. DOT's Federal Highway Administration ([fhwa.dot.gov](https://www.fhwa.dot.gov)), if your number is higher, a 10,000-mile lease is a trap, and we'll steer you to finance or a higher mileage tier instead. Second, if you do lease, take care of the small dings during the term, not at the end, when the lease-return inspector prices them.

The flip side: leasing genuinely wins for low-mileage drivers who like staying current on safety tech, and for business use where the structure can make sense, talk to your own tax advisor on that, not us. We're not anti-lease. We're anti-surprise.
